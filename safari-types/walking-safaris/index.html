Safari Type 01

Walking Safaris

Slow, quiet and close to the ground. The purest way to read the bush.

Out of the vehicle, the bush rearranges itself. A track in the sand becomes a story; a guide’s raised hand stops everyone mid-step. Walking safaris rarely deliver the most sightings, but they deliver the most understanding.

What a walking safari is

A walking safari is a guided walk through a wildlife area, led by a qualified walking guide and, in most countries, an armed scout or ranger. Walks usually start at first light, when it is cool and animals are active, and last three to four hours. You cover only a few kilometres. The point is not distance but attention: tracks, dung, birdsong, alarm calls, the plants animals eat and the trees they shelter under.

The modern walking safari was pioneered in Zambia’s Luangwa Valley in the 1950s and 60s by conservationist Norman Carr, and the valley remains one of the best places in Africa to do it.

What you will and won’t see

On foot you approach big animals far more cautiously than in a vehicle — wildlife that ignores a Land Cruiser reacts to a person walking. Guides keep a respectful distance from elephants, buffalo and predators, and you should not expect to walk up to lions. What you gain is everything a vehicle drives past: the small, the subtle and the sensory. Many travelers say their single most memorable moment came on foot.

Is it safe?

Walking with a properly trained guide in a well-run camp has an excellent safety record. The safety comes from the guide’s training and judgement, and from a simple set of rules you are briefed on before you leave: walk in single file, stay quiet, never run, and follow instructions immediately. In Zambia and Zimbabwe walking guides go through demanding, multi-year licensing, and Zimbabwe’s professional guide qualification is widely regarded as one of the toughest in Africa.

Who it suits

  • Travelers on a second or third safari who want something deeper.
  • First-timers who already know they prefer quiet and detail to ticking off species.
  • Photographers interested in landscape and intimacy rather than long-lens close-ups.

You need moderate fitness — walks are slow, but on uneven ground and in heat. Most camps set a minimum age between 12 and 16.

Where to walk

  • South Luangwa, Zambia — the home of the walking safari, with dedicated bush camps and multi-day walking trails in the dry season.
  • Mana Pools, Zimbabwe — open floodplain woodland and some of Africa’s most respected walking guides.
  • Lower Zambezi, Zambia — combine morning walks with afternoons on the river.
  • Ruaha and Nyerere, Tanzania — walking in large, little-visited parks.

When to go

In southern Africa, walking is best in the dry season, roughly June to October, when grass is short and visibility good. Many walking-focused camps close during the rains. See when to go for month-by-month detail.

Frequently asked questions

How long is a walking safari?
Most walks last three to four hours in the early morning. Some camps offer multi-day walking trails where you walk between simple fly camps.
Do I need to be very fit?
No. Walks are slow and you stop often, but you should be comfortable walking on uneven ground in warm weather for a few hours.
Can children go on walking safaris?
Usually only older children. Most camps set a minimum age between 12 and 16 for walking.
